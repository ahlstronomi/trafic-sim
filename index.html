Why the throughput matches

The gate is a hard, fixed-rate server: it releases at most one car every T seconds, whatever is going on upstream. As soon as a queue exists, the gate is the only thing limiting flow, so both strategies send through the same cars per minute, and the total time cars spend waiting is the same too. The strategies only decide where the waiting happens: early merge stacks it in one lane, zipper spreads it across two, so the zipper queue is about half as long in meters.

Panel C shows that queue-jumping is a transfer, not a saving: each late merger skips the line, and every car behind the slot it takes waits one gate interval longer. The gate's rate, and so the total, is unchanged — only who pays changes (compare the late mergers' wait with everyone else's in C's tiles).

Friction is where the strategies can part ways. If the act of merging makes the gate slower, the strategy whose merging coincides with a release pays for it — turn either friction slider up to see which one wins under which assumption. C can pay both kinds: early-merge friction for its upstream lane changes and zipper friction when a late merger cuts in at the gate.

Model notes and simplifications
  • Cars are 4.5 m long and keep at least 7 m front-to-front, so a stopped queue takes 7 m per car. Free speed is 15 m/s (54 km/h). Speed changes are instantaneous: a car moves to the position its leader allows and never overlaps it. There is no acceleration limit, which keeps the gate (not engine power) as the only bottleneck.
  • Arrivals: the gap between cars is drawn from a gamma distribution with mean 1/rate and the chosen coefficient of variation (0 gives perfectly even gaps, 1 is Poisson-like, 2 is very bursty). Each car picks the through lane or the closing lane 50/50 from a separate seeded stream, so changing the irregularity does not change which lane cars use. Both roads receive the identical sequence.
  • Gate: one car per T seconds, work-conserving (idle gate lets the next car through at once). A car waiting for the gate creeps forward so it arrives exactly when the gate reopens, which is why queues creep rather than lurch.
  • Early merge: the closing lane ends at 115 m. A car in it joins the single file when it reaches the merge zone (70–115 m) or gets within 30 m of the back of the queue, whichever comes first. It takes a slot only where the car behind is already 7 m back; otherwise it drops in behind that car. Letting a car in costs the file one slot, as in reality.
  • Late mergers (panel C): every Nth car arriving in the closing lane (N from the slider, counted in arrival order) ignores the merge sign and drives on to the gate; all other cars behave as in A. At the gate the late merger is treated like a zipper lane: when both it and the file head can reach the line in time, the gate alternates, so a lone late merger takes the very next slot. A late merger stuck behind a car that is still waiting to merge is not eligible until that car has moved over.
  • Zipper: both lanes run to the gate. Whenever both lanes have a car that can reach the gate in time, the gate takes the lane that did not go last; otherwise whoever is there goes. Cars are never held back for an absent partner.
  • Wait = time from arrival to passing the gate minus the 16.7 s an unobstructed car needs. Queue = cars moving slower than 90% of free speed in the unbroken slow region behind the gate (plus cars waiting to merge at its tail); its length is measured from the gate to the rearmost of them.
  • Friction: for an affected release the gate takes T ÷ (1 − friction) seconds. Zipper: the released car had waited in the queue and came from the other lane than the previous car (a genuine interleave). Early merge: a lane change is waiting, in progress, or finished within the last T seconds anywhere upstream. “Releases while merging” shows how often each condition fired.
  • Ratio view: each queue ÷ the zipper queue, drawn on a log scale and only while both queues hold at least 3 cars, since the ratio of two tiny queues is noise. The “typical” figure is the geometric mean over the visible window.
  • If a queue grows past the left edge, cars keep arriving and wait off-screen; a badge counts them. In the early-merge panel, closing-lane cars arriving while the queue is off-screen are placed at the back of the single file (they merged before reaching the view) rather than slotting in at the entry.
